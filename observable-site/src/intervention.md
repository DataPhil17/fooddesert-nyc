# Intervention Analysis

## How Many Grocery Stores Does NYC Need?

This analysis estimates the number of additional SNAP-authorized grocery stores required for every underserved neighborhood to reach Good Access status. The calculation is based on the formula weight of the snap_grocery_per_10k term: each additional SNAP grocery store per 10,000 residents adds 8.0 points to a neighborhood's Food Access Score.

59 neighborhoods across NYC — 13 Food Deserts and 46 At Risk — require intervention to reach Good Access status.

---

## Two-Stage Framework for Food Desert Neighborhoods

Food Desert neighborhoods receive a two-stage intervention estimate.

**Stage 1 — Escape Food Desert status** — the minimum number of stores needed to cross the At Risk threshold (score 25.2). This represents an achievable near-term target.

**Stage 2 — Reach Good Access** — the total number of stores needed to reach full Good Access status (score 54.7). This is the longer-term remediation target.

At Risk neighborhoods receive a single estimate: stores needed to reach Good Access directly.

## Priority Neighborhoods

Among Food Desert neighborhoods, Throggs Neck (population 46,457) and Bayside (population 49,912) serve the largest populations with the greatest documented need. Murray Hill's Food Desert classification despite a $153,000 median income highlights a different kind of gap: the near-absence of SNAP-authorized grocery stores in a neighborhood where residents shop at non-SNAP specialty retailers.

Among At Risk neighborhoods, those combining large populations with the most stores needed to reach Good Access represent the highest-priority cases for sustained investment.

## NYC's City-Owned Grocery Initiative

New York City is actively rolling out city-owned grocery stores in underserved neighborhoods. Mayor Mamdani's administration announced the initiative with the first locations expected to open in 2026, targeting neighborhoods with documented food access gaps. This analysis provides a data-driven framework for evaluating which neighborhoods should be prioritized and how many stores are needed to produce a measurable improvement in food access scores.

## Caveats

Store counts are directional indicators of relative need rather than precise policy targets. The relative ordering of neighborhoods by stores needed is more meaningful than the absolute numbers. Adding grocery stores would also improve the food balance ratio and potentially reduce convenience store dominance, meaning actual remediation may require fewer stores than estimated. Optimal placement within a neighborhood requires street-network analysis and foot traffic data not available in this dataset.
